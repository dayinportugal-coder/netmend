How to reply on X to grow

Replies are how a lot of people actually discover a new account: they see a useful comment under a post they already care about. That only works if the reply is a contribution. “Great post! 🔥” plus a link to your service is not a contribution. It is litter.

Nobody can honestly promise that replies will grow an account, including us. What you can do is become the person whose comments are worth reading. Over time, some of those readers will follow you. Some will not. The work is still worth doing if you care about the topic.

Pick conversations you know something about. Stay for a beat after you reply, in case the author answers. Do not mass-reply 40 posts with a variant of the same sentence. That pattern is obvious, and it is how accounts get muted.

Replies that add something

  • Add a fact or a step the original post did not have, from work you have actually done.
  • Ask a follow-up that is specific — a question the author can answer in one or two lines, not “thoughts?”.
  • Disagree with a reason, and a limit. Name the part you disagree with. Do not attack the person.
  • Tell a short, relevant example, clearly, without hijacking the thread into your memoir or your product.
  • Leave the pitch out. If your profile and your posts explain what you do, people who want it can find it. A link-dump under a stranger’s post does the opposite of growth: it teaches people to hide you.
  • Credit and move on. If you are only saying “this is right, and here is a smaller version of the same point”, consider whether the reply is needed at all.

Example replies (fictional parents in italics in the label)

Add a fact · example
Original (paraphrase): “Bank feeds are enough; you do not need bookkeeping.”

Reply: “A bank feed is useful, and it is not the same thing as a reviewed set of books. Transfers still have to be matched, and the odd payments still have to be asked about. The feed is the input, not the review.”
Specific follow-up · example
Original: a founder describes hiding a feature behind a flag.

Reply: “Did you turn the flag off for new workspaces only, or for existing ones too? We did existing-only first, and support still got tickets from old accounts that expected the old button.”
Disagree, with a reason · example
Original: “Every consultant should post revenue in public.”

Reply: “I would not make that a rule. A dated decision is often more useful than a number, and some clients would be identifiable from a graph even if you never name them. If you do post a number, it should be one you can stand behind in a due-diligence room.”
Short example, no pitch · example
Original: a thread about onboarding drop-off.

Reply: “We had the same stall: people did not know whether to add a client or an invoice first. We hid the other buttons until one client existed. I will not pretend that is a universal fix, but it was the right fix for that screen.”
What not to post · example
Do not write: “Great thread! I help founders with onboarding — link in bio, 20% off this week.”

If you have something useful to say about onboarding, say the useful thing, and stop. The bio can do the rest.
When not to reply · example
A stranger is posting about a bereavement, a medical crisis, or a fight you do not understand.

You do not need a clever contribution. You can keep scrolling. Growth is not a reason to walk into that thread.
Very short useful reply · example
“The official page for this deadline is on the tax authority’s own site, and it is worth checking your own account against it. I would not take a date from a screenshot in a thread.”
After they answer · example
If the author replies to you, answer once, clearly, and do not drag the thread into a tour of your product.

A short “that makes sense — we only did it for new workspaces, which is why I asked” is enough. You can always write your own post later.

These are original example drafts, written for this page, for a fictional professional. They are not real posts from a real account, and they are not a promise of reach, clients or sales. Adapt the structure; replace every fact with one you can stand behind.

Time and tone

A small, repeatable habit beats a weekend binge: 15–20 minutes on a handful of posts you can actually add to. Mute words and accounts that pull you into fights. Do not pile on. Do not reply to a typo with a courtroom tone.

FAQ

Is it acceptable to reply under large accounts every day?

If you consistently add something, yes. If you are camping there with a slogan, no. People notice, including the author.

Should I tag the author?

They will usually see the reply anyway if it is on their post. Extra tags, especially of uninvolved people, look like a grab for attention.

Can replies replace writing my own posts?

They can start discovery. They are a weak substitute for having a profile and a few posts that show how you think. Do both, lightly.
