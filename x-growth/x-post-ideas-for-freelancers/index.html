X post ideas for freelancers

A freelance feed that only says “available for work” or “just hit my best month” is hard to hire from. The posts that get serious replies are usually about scope, rates, process, or a piece of work you can actually show.

This is for independent designers, developers, writers, illustrators, editors and similar trades. It is not a promise that posting will replace a sales pipeline. If you mention money, use figures you would also put in a proposal. If you mention a client, you need their permission, or you talk about the work in a way that does not identify them.

Useful freelance posts tend to teach a buyer how to brief you, or teach a peer how you run the job. “I am taking on two more retainers in January” can work as a last line. It is a weak first line.

What is worth posting

  • A scope boundary you now write into contracts, and the mess that taught you to add it.
  • How you price a common job, in ranges, with what is in and out. Not a fake “I charge $10k for a logo” flex unless that is true.
  • A before-and-after of the brief, not only of the pixels: the questions you asked, the constraint you added.
  • A process post: discovery, draft, revision cap, delivery files, what happens if the project pauses.
  • A red-flag list for both sides — late briefs, unpaid “quick calls”, revision loops with no cap.
  • A finished piece with the constraint (deadline, brand rules, accessibility, budget) that shaped it.

Example posts you can adapt

Concrete-fact hook · example
My website redesigns start at $4,800 for a five-page marketing site, written in the proposal as: homepage, three inner pages, a contact form, and two rounds of revisions.

CMS training, extra templates and a blog index are quoted separately. I would rather say this here than discover it on a call.
Question + list · example
If you are hiring a freelancer this month, put these four lines in the brief before you ask for a price:

1. What already exists (files, CMS, brand).
2. What “done” looks like, in deliverable files.
3. The drop-dead date, and why.
4. Who signs off — one name, not a committee.

I can quote from that. I cannot quote from “we need a refresh”.
Short twist · example
I stopped offering unlimited revisions.

Unlimited sounds generous. It usually means the brief was never finished. I now write “two rounds” in the statement of work, and a day rate after that.
Lessons from work · example
Three projects that went wrong last year had the same first week: a kickoff call, no written brief, and a “we will know it when we see it” on taste.

I now send a one-page brief back to the client for a yes before I open the file. If they cannot approve the brief, we do not start.
Process · example
For a copywriting job I do this, in order:

1. A 45-minute kickoff and a written outline you approve.
2. First draft, then one structured revision.
3. Final files in Google Docs and Markdown.

If legal needs a pass, we add it at the outline stage, not after the “final” draft.
Red flags · example
I no longer take a “quick call” with no agenda from a stranger who found me on X.

Send five lines: the job, the deadline, the budget range, and whether this is a one-off or a retainer. If that feels like too much writing, we are not a match.
Very short hook · example
A low rate with a vague scope is not a bargain.

It is how both sides end up resenting the work.
Work sample post · example
Last month I rewrote a pricing page for a B2B tool. The constraint was: no new screenshots, no invented case studies, and the three plans had to stay as they were.

Most of the work was cutting adjectives and putting the limits (seats, log retention, support hours) in the same order on every plan. That is the job, even when it does not look flashy.

These are original example drafts, written for this page, for a fictional professional. They are not real posts from a real account, and they are not a promise of reach, clients or sales. Adapt the structure; replace every fact with one you can stand behind.

What to skip

Do not post someone else’s invoice or a mocked “$32k month” screenshot. Do not name a difficult client. Do not use a competitor’s client as a public case study. And do not turn every thread into a pitch; if people like your process posts, they can follow the link in your bio.

FAQ

Should I post my rates publicly?

If your rates are stable and you are comfortable with peers seeing them, a clear range saves everyone time. If they vary a lot by client, describe how you price instead of a single number.

How do I show work without naming the client?

Talk about the constraint and the artefact: the page, the illustration, the feature. Ask the client first if the brand would be recognisable. When in doubt, wait for written permission.

Does posting every day bring clients?

Sometimes a post starts a conversation; often it does not. Treat X as a public notebook and a place to be findable, not as a replacement for direct outreach you already do.
