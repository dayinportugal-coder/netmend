X post ideas for authors

A feed that only says “my book is out” is a catalogue. Authors who get something useful out of X usually post a line that stands on its own, a process note, a research detail, or a clear, dated piece of publication news — and they do not apologise for writing, or for asking once, clearly, where the book can be found.

This is for novelists, non-fiction writers, poets and essayists. Do not invent blurbs, bestseller ranks or reviews. Do not paste someone else’s text as if it were yours. If you quote a review, keep it accurate and within fair use or the reviewer’s permission. Be honest about whether a book is self-published, traditionally published, or not yet out.

A good writing post often does one of two jobs: it is a complete thought a stranger can enjoy, or it is a window on the work (research, structure, a constraint) that does not require the reader to have bought the book.

What to post besides the cover

  • A self-contained line or paragraph you are willing to stand behind without the rest of the chapter.
  • A process post: hours, drafts, how you research, how you cut.
  • A research or reporting detail (for non-fiction) with a source when a fact is cited.
  • A publication fact: publication date, where it is sold, whether there is an audiobook, a launch event with a place and time.
  • A reading or craft note that is yours, not a dunk on a successful stranger.
  • A once-in-a-while, clear ask (pre-order, a review if the reader already finished it), not a daily lunge.

Example posts you can adapt

Very short hook · example
The first draft is where I find out what the book is not.

The second is where I stop pretending the discarded plot was a secret strength.
Concrete-fact hook · example
The book comes out on 3 March, in paperback and ebook, and the audiobook follows in April, read by Jordan Hale, if that date holds.

The pre-order link is in my bio. I will post it again closer to the date, not every morning until then.
Question + list · example
When I get stuck on a chapter, I do these three things before I invent a new plot:

1. Write the scene in six sentences, as if to a friend.
2. Cut any sentence that explains a feeling the action already shows.
3. Read it out loud once, standing up.

If it still dies, the problem is usually earlier, in the previous chapter, not in a missing twist.
Research · example
A detail in chapter four comes from the official report of the 1953 flood, not from a documentary I half-remembered.

I put the report in the acknowledgements. If I got something wrong, I want to hear it, in writing, and I will correct it if I can.
Short twist · example
I do not write every day.

I write most weekday mornings for 90 minutes, and I take the school holidays off more often than a productivity thread would like. The book still got finished. Your rhythm can be dull and still be real.
Craft · example
If a paragraph exists only to show that I did the research, I cut it.

The reader should feel that I know the place. They should not have to watch me unpack the archive onto the page.
Ask, once · example
If you have already finished the book and you were going to leave a review, it does help — on the shop you bought it from, in your own words.

If you have not read it, please do not post a review. That is the whole ask.
Process · example
This week I cut 3,200 words from the middle of the book, including a character I liked.

She was doing work the plot no longer needed. I have saved her in a notes file, which is where most of my darlings now live. The chapter is shorter, and the next one starts sooner.

These are original example drafts, written for this page, for a fictional professional. They are not real posts from a real account, and they are not a promise of reach, clients or sales. Adapt the structure; replace every fact with one you can stand behind.

FAQ

How often may I mention the book?

Often enough that a new follower can find it, rarely enough that a regular is not being sold to in every post. A pinned post and a bio link do a lot of that work.

Should I post drafts?

Only if you are willing to see them circulate. A finished line is usually safer than a raw paragraph you may later dislike.

Is X useful for fiction at all?

Sometimes, for process, community and announcements. It is not a replacement for the work, the editor, or the places your readers already are.
